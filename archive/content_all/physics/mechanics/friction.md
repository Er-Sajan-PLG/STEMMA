name: Friction
domain: physics
status: draft
definition: A contact force that opposes the relative motion (or tendency of relative
  motion) between two surfaces in contact. Friction acts parallel to the contact surface
  and opposite to the direction of motion or attempted motion.
symbol: f
unit: newton (N)
equation: f = μ·N (for sliding friction; μ = coefficient of friction, N = normal force)
examples:
- A box is harder to start moving than to keep sliding.
- Tyres grip the road; without friction a car cannot brake or corner.
key_experiments:
- 'Block on a surface: measure the force to start it moving versus the force to keep
  it moving steadily.'
- Measuring the force needed to start sliding an object (static friction) vs. keep
  it sliding (kinetic friction).
- Investigating how surface type and normal force affect friction.
common_misconceptions:
- Friction always opposes motion (it opposes relative motion — it can also cause motion,
  e.g., walking).
- Friction depends on contact area (for sliding friction, it depends primarily on
  normal force and materials).
- Friction is always undesirable (without friction, we couldn't walk, drive, or hold
  objects).
- Smooth surfaces have no friction (all surfaces have some friction; even "smooth"
  surfaces have microscopic irregularities).
learning_objectives:
- Identify the direction of frictional force in physical situations.
- Distinguish between static and kinetic friction.
- Explain factors affecting friction.
- Analyze forces on objects with friction present.
- Solve problems involving friction on horizontal and inclined surfaces.
real_world_applications:
- Walking and driving (friction provides traction).
- Braking systems.
- Writing (friction between pen and paper).
- Matches lighting.
- Wear on machinery (friction as a source of energy loss).

## Notes

Friction is both essential and problematic. Without it, we couldn't function; with it,
we lose energy to heat. Understanding friction is prerequisite to realistic mechanics problems.
The distinction between static and kinetic friction explains why it's harder to start pushing
something than to keep pushing it.
